How to Write Better AI Image Generation Prompts

November 12, 2026 · PromptVerse Editorial Team · 10 min read

A good AI image prompt does more than name an object. It gives the image generator enough visual direction to understand the subject, setting, composition, lighting and overall mood. This guide explains a repeatable method you can use with different image-generation tools.

1. Start with a clear subject

Put the most important subject near the beginning of your prompt. Be specific about what it is, what it is doing and, when relevant, who or what should be visible. “A person” leaves many decisions open; “a ceramic artist shaping a clay bowl at a wooden workbench” gives the model a much clearer starting point.

If the image needs one main subject, say so. If several objects matter, describe their relationship rather than listing them without context.

2. Add useful visual details

Choose details that change what the viewer will actually see: clothing, materials, colors, textures, location, weather or time of day. For a product image, mention the product finish and background. For a landscape, describe the terrain, season and atmosphere.

Practical example

Instead of “a coffee cup,” try “a handmade matte-white ceramic coffee cup on a walnut table, with a little steam rising beside an open notebook.”

3. Describe the composition

Composition tells the generator how the scene should be arranged. Useful terms include close-up, wide shot, eye-level view, overhead view, centered subject, negative space and shallow depth of field. Pick the terms that support your intended result; adding every photography term can make the prompt less clear.

For a website banner, for example, request a subject positioned on the right with clean negative space on the left for headline text. For a profile portrait, a close-up with the face in sharp focus may be more suitable.

4. Choose lighting and mood

Lighting strongly affects the feeling of an image. Soft window light can feel calm and natural; dramatic side lighting creates contrast; golden-hour sunlight feels warm; diffused studio lighting is often useful for clean product photography.

Describe the light source and mood together when possible, such as “soft morning window light with gentle shadows” or “moody blue-hour lighting with subtle reflections.”

5. Specify a visual style

Choose a style that matches the purpose: editorial photography, minimalist product photography, watercolor illustration, 3D product render, architectural visualization or a clean vector graphic. You can also define a restrained color palette, such as warm neutrals or cool blues.

Avoid stacking unrelated styles. “Photorealistic watercolor 3D vector oil painting” sends mixed signals. Select one primary direction and add only compatible supporting details.

6. Before-and-after prompt examples

Basic prompt

A modern desk setup.

More descriptive prompt

A minimalist home-office desk with a slim laptop, a small green plant and a warm desk lamp, photographed from a slightly elevated angle, soft daylight, neutral beige and walnut palette, clean editorial interior photography, generous empty space.

Basic prompt

A mountain landscape.

More descriptive prompt

A wide alpine valley at sunrise, layered mountain peaks in the distance, a narrow river reflecting pale gold light, low morning mist, natural colors, wide landscape composition, realistic travel photography.

The improved versions work because they specify visible choices. They are starting points, not guarantees: different models may interpret the same words differently.

7. Use this reusable prompt template

Fill in the parts that matter for your image. Remove any field that does not apply.

[Main subject] + [action or pose] + [setting/background] + [important visual details] + [composition/camera angle] + [lighting] + [visual style] + [mood/color palette] + [aspect ratio, if supported]

Example: “A red bicycle leaning against a brick wall on a quiet city street, three-quarter side view, soft overcast daylight, muted cinematic colors, realistic lifestyle photography, vertical composition.”

8. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Being too vague: add concrete details that affect the image.
  • Adding conflicting instructions: keep style, lighting and mood consistent.
  • Overloading the prompt: prioritize the few details that matter most.
  • Ignoring the intended format: specify portrait, landscape or square framing when the tool supports it.
  • Expecting perfect text in images: image generators can struggle with exact lettering; add important typography later in a design editor.

9. A simple testing workflow

  1. Write one clear prompt with a subject, setting and visual direction.
  2. Generate a small first batch and identify what is working.
  3. Change one part at a time, such as lighting or camera angle.
  4. Keep useful versions so you can compare results.
  5. Check the final image for unwanted objects, distorted details and incorrect composition.

Changing one variable at a time makes it easier to understand which words influence the result. Keep in mind that generation tools are probabilistic, so repeating an identical prompt may still produce different images.

Key takeaway

Strong image prompts combine a clear subject with purposeful details about composition, lighting and style. Start simple, review the output and refine one element at a time.
